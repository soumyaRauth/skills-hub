Human Empathy

Design with empathy. Start by asking how people feel while they use what you build, understand their needs more deeply than anyone else does, then build for those people. The same standard for a sign-up form and a PDF book: does this leave the person understood, supported, capable, and closer to what they came for?

Soumya Rauth · Creator & maintainer
npx skills add soumyaRauth/skills-hub --skill human-empathy

The problem

it works ≠ people can finish it · it looks good ≠ people can read it

Most reviews check the artifact. The failures that cost you people live in their experience, and each of these passes a functional check: 

sign-up      a server hiccup erases all ten fields the person typed on a phone
sign-up      "Something went wrong" for an email that already has an account
book, ch. 3  nine new concepts in the first listing; HTTP explained last
book, ch. 3  the listing does not run on the versions chapter 1 names
the PDF      a coding ligature draws --reload as a dash; beginners type the dash

The person meeting any of these rarely thinks the product is broken. They think I’m not good at this, and they leave.

No slash command needed. Installed, it is loaded when a request is about people’s experience and stays out of the way otherwise. How the skills work together →

Stays quietA label change, a typo, a grammar fix, a spacing tweak, or any single edit nobody framed as an experience question. A typo in a book is a typo, not a book review.

How it works

the person  →  their situation and needs  →  the real experience (run it, read it, render it)
   →  walk it as them  →  findings with evidence levels  →  root causes
   →  scores from a published rubric  →  specific changes  →  re-check

Every claim carries one of four labels, so you always know what is known:

LabelMeans
VerifiedObserved in the product or document, or in research you supplied
SupportedAn inference from what was observed plus named, established research or convention
HypothesisPlausible, untested, and could be wrong either way
PreferenceTaste, with no evidence it changes the experience
How someone feels is never “verified” without real people saying or showing it. The skill writes what a design is likely to cause, explains the mechanism, and tells you how to find out.

Products and books, one framework

Products

Purpose and value · discovery, onboarding, first value, core tasks, recovery, returning, leaving · forms, controls, feedback · loading, empty, error and success states · microcopy · emotional experience · accessibility as lived · privacy, consent and deceptive patterns · whether each feature serves a real need.

Books

The reader and the promise · architecture and chapter order · learning progression and cognitive load · explanation, examples and practice · editorial quality without imposing a style · technical accuracy, with the code executed · typography, layout and code on rendered pages · PDF navigation and accessibility · publication readiness.

Both are scored on the same ten dimensions, one to five, against anchors published in the skill:

1–5Human Understanding · Emotional Experience · Cognitive Simplicity · Clarity and Communication · Personal Relevance · Accessibility and Inclusion · Respect for the User · Practical Value · Experience Consistency · Trustworthiness
OverallThe median of the scored dimensions, rounded down, capped at 2 when the experience deceives people, excludes them or cannot deliver its promise. Fewer than six dimensions scored means insufficient evidence, not a guess.

Worked examples ship with the skill: a registration flow, a technical book for beginners, a chapter rebuilt, an attractive, unreadable PDF, and staying quiet.

What it will not do

  • Invent people. No made-up interviews, reader quotes, personas presented as research, statistics, studies or citations
  • Claim validation it did not do. A change is never “validated with users” unless people tested it; the report names the test that would
  • Trust code that looks right. Examples are executed, checked against documentation, or marked unverified
  • Judge a page from its text. PDF pages are rendered and looked at; when nothing can render them, it says so
  • Manufacture precision. No percentages, no decimals, no score where the evidence is insufficient
  • Take over your work. A book’s audience, promise or chapter order, a product’s data collection or consent: proposed, then your decision
  • Weaken a control to remove friction. A password policy stays; how it is explained changes
  • Flatter. When the design or the manuscript fails people, it says so, with evidence

Using it

  • “Walk through our sign-up as a first-time user and tell me what it’s like.”
  • “Should we add streaks to the habit tracker?”
  • “Review my Python book for beginners. Readers say chapter 3 loses them.”
  • “Is book.pdf easy to read on a laptop?”
  • “Fix chapter 3 so beginners can follow it.”
  • “Help me plan a book on SQL for data analysts.”
  • “Is this manuscript ready to publish?”

Limitations

  • It cannot know how people feel. It finds what is likely to cause a feeling, says why, and recommends the research that would tell you
  • Without the running product it reads the code and says so; visual and timing findings are then inferred
  • Visual PDF review needs something that renders pages: poppler, PyMuPDF, Ghostscript or a headless browser. Without one, it limits itself to what the source proves
  • Conformance with WCAG, PDF/UA or privacy law is not its call; it hands the evidence to Standards Compass
  • Judgements about natural phrasing in languages other than English carry lower confidence unless the project or the author supplies conventions
